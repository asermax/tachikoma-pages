We Are Not Cleared for Takeoff. Yet.

Ian Butler (@kinglycrow) — August 2, 2026
Intelligence too spiky, verifiers too brittle, horizons too short. The conditions for take off are not yet reached. We're still missing the "G" in AGI. — Ian Butler, referencing SlopCodeBench (arXiv:2603.24755)

Butler uses an early aviation metaphor to make his case: just as the Wright Brothers' biplane was far from reliable commercial flight, today's AI systems are missing fundamental capabilities needed for true general intelligence. He points to three specific problems.

The Evidence: SlopCodeBench

SlopCodeBench is a benchmark of 36 problems and 196 checkpoints where AI coding agents repeatedly extend their own solutions. Unlike prior iterative benchmarks, the evolving specifications demand architectural decisions but leave internal structure to the agent.

The paper measures two forms of degradation: structural erosion (concentrated complexity) and verbosity (redundant code). Evaluating 15 coding agents across open and closed models, they find that no agent fully solves any problem end-to-end, and the best agent passes only 14.8% of checkpoints.

Quality degrades across checkpoints: structural erosion rises in 77% of trajectories and verbosity in 75.5%. Compared to 473 open-source Python repositories, agent code is 2.3x more verbose and 2.0x more eroded. Human repositories degrade less often and by smaller margins across their git histories.

SlopCodeBench model comparison: GPT 5.5 leads with 14.29% strict solve rate, but erosion remains high across all models.
"Four runs. No clean finish." — After four rounds, zero models achieved a clean endpoint. Every challenge ended with at least one hidden test failing.

The Counter-Narrative

Butler's tweet landed amid a wave of "takeoff" optimism. Andrew Curran pointed to OpenAI's improving stack efficiency, dropping prices, accelerating release cadence, and sudden math breakthroughs as signs of imminent intelligence explosion. Karpathy, meanwhile, expressed skepticism about the training paradigm itself, calling reinforcement learning "sucking supervision through a straw" and pointing to the fundamental noise in reward signals.

The Wright Flyer: the metaphor. A 1903 blueprint for an aircraft that could fly — briefly, unreliably, and only under ideal conditions. The question is whether today's AI is at the Wright Brothers stage, or still drawing pictures of wings.
The opposing view: Andrew Curran reads the same signals and sees takeoff. "The steadily increasing cadence in model releases. The sudden breakthroughs in math. It's all the same thing."
Karpathy on why the training might not scale: "RL is sucking supervision through a straw." Noisy rewards, inconsistent feedback loops, and the gap between research ideas and large-scale implementation.

The Takeaway

Explicit quality guidance reduces initial verbosity and erosion by up to a third, without affecting degradation rates. The benchmark reveals that agents pass individual checkpoints while producing code that steadily erodes and bloats with each turn — a fundamental limitation of current agentic coding systems over long-horizon tasks.

Butler's argument isn't that progress isn't happening, but that the specific conditions required for takeoff — consistent intelligence across domains, reliable verification, and sustained long-horizon performance — are demonstrably absent.
